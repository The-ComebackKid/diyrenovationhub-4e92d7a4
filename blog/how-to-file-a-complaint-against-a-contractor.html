How to File a Complaint Against a Contractor (And Which Agency Actually Gets Results)

By DIY Renovation Hub · September 2026 · 8 min read

Most homeowners' first move after a bad contractor experience is a one-star review. It feels good, it does almost nothing, and it can even expose you to a defamation claim if you overstate what happened. There are four real complaint paths that can actually get you a result — money back, license discipline, or both — and most people only ever use one of them, if any. Here's what each one does, what it doesn't do, and the order that puts the most pressure on the contractor at once.

1. State Contractor Licensing Board

Every state that licenses contractors has a board that can investigate, and the complaint is usually free and filed online. What it's good for: contractors care about their license, and a formal complaint puts one on record — repeat complaints can lead to suspension or revocation, which is real leverage even before a hearing happens. Some boards also offer free mediation or arbitration between you and the contractor as a first step.

What it's not good for on its own: the board's job is professional discipline, not debt collection. It generally won't force the contractor to pay you directly — that's what the bond claim (below) and small claims court are for. File this complaint anyway; it's free, it's fast, and it's part of the paper trail everything else builds on.

Find Yours Search "[your state] contractor licensing board complaint" — nearly every state's board has a dedicated online complaint portal. If your state doesn't license general contractors at all (a handful don't), skip to the attorney general and small claims routes below.

2. The Contractor's Surety Bond

Licensed contractors in most states are required to carry a surety bond — money set aside specifically to pay consumers when something goes wrong. Your licensing board complaint or license lookup will usually show the bonding company and bond number. You file a claim directly with that bonding company, submitting your contract, payment records, and documentation of the unfinished or defective work. Bonds have payout caps (commonly $10,000-$25,000, sometimes less) and the bond is shared among every claimant against that contractor that year, so this isn't unlimited money — but it's often the fastest actual cash recovery on this list, frequently resolved in weeks rather than months.

3. State Attorney General / Consumer Protection Division

Every state has a consumer protection division, usually inside the attorney general's office, that enforces the state's deceptive trade practices act. This path is strongest when there's a pattern — false statements, bait-and-switch pricing, taking a deposit and never starting work, using unlicensed subs while representing they're licensed. One complaint may just get logged. But AG offices track complaint volume per business, and a contractor with a growing file can end up facing an actual enforcement action, sometimes with restitution built in. File here even if you expect nothing immediate — you may be the complaint that tips the pattern.

4. Small Claims Court

This is the one path that can order the contractor to pay you directly, and it's built for exactly this kind of dispute — no attorney required in most states, filing fees are typically under $100, and jurisdictional limits commonly range from $5,000 to $25,000 depending on the state, which covers the overwhelming majority of residential disputes. Bring your contract, every payment record, your notice-to-cure letter if you sent one, photos, and (ideally) a written estimate from another contractor pricing out what it actually costs to finish or fix the work — that number is usually your damages figure.

File in the Right Order File the licensing board complaint and the bond claim first — they're free or fast and cost you nothing to run in parallel. File the AG complaint alongside them. Reserve small claims for the money you still haven't recovered once the bond claim resolves, since a court will want to know what other recovery you've already pursued or received.

What to Gather Before You File Anything

  • The signed contract, including any change orders.
  • Every payment record — canceled checks, card statements, receipts.
  • Every text, email, and any voicemail transcripts.
  • Dated photos and video of the work at every stage.
  • A written estimate from a licensed contractor to complete or correct the work, if you've gotten one.

The same file works for all four complaints. Build it once, submit it everywhere.
