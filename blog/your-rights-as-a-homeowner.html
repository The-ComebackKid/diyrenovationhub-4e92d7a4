Your Rights as a Homeowner: What Every Building Inspector Won't Tell You

By DIY Renovation Hub · May 2025 · 12 min read

The International Residential Code is public law. Your permit application is a legal document. Your contractor's license is a bonded, regulated instrument backed by your state. Most homeowners never learn what rights and protections they actually hold — until something goes catastrophically wrong on a job. This article puts those rights in your hands before you need them.

Right #1: You Can Read the Code Book Too

Contractors and inspectors operate on a knowledge gap. The gap is artificial. The IRC, NEC, and plumbing codes your jurisdiction adopts are published law — and you're allowed to read every word. When an inspector fails your framing, the correction notice cites a code section. Look it up. When a contractor says "code requires it" to justify a change order, ask for the section number. If they can't produce one, you've learned something important about that contractor.

Code Ref Free read-only access to the IRC and IBC is available through the International Code Council's public access portal. The NEC (NFPA 70) is viewable free through NFPA's website with a basic account. You never have to take anyone's word for what "the code says."

Right #2: You Can Pull Your Own Permit

In most jurisdictions, an owner-occupant can apply for their own building permit for work on their own home. You don't need a contractor to get one. Under IRC R105, permits are issued for the work described in the application — the building department reviews your plans, and their inspectors check the work at required stages. That inspection sequence is not an obstacle. It's a free layer of quality control performed by someone with no financial stake in cutting corners.

Two warnings that come with this right:

  • Whoever pulls the permit owns the responsibility. If you pull the permit and hire helpers, you're the responsible party for code compliance.
  • Never let a contractor work under a permit you pulled for their scope. Some unlicensed operators push homeowners to pull the permit so they can work in the shadows. If a contractor asks you to do this, walk away — it usually means they can't pull one themselves.

Right #3: Permits Are Public Records

Any permit ever pulled on your property — or a property you're thinking about buying — is a public record. So are the inspection results. Before you buy a flipped house, pull the permit history. A finished basement, a new electrical service, an addition with no permits on file tells you exactly what kind of work you're inheriting: uninspected work. Unpermitted work can trigger fines, forced removal, and insurance claim denials, and it follows the property, not the person who did it.

Right #4: The License and the Bond Exist for You

A contractor's license isn't a business decoration. In license states, it's tied to a surety bond and often to insurance minimums — recovery mechanisms that exist specifically for consumers. If a licensed contractor abandons your job or damages your property, you can file against the bond. If they're unlicensed, that mechanism doesn't exist, and in many states an unlicensed contractor can't even sue you to enforce their own contract.

  • Verify license status through your state licensing board's online lookup — every state that licenses contractors has one.
  • Get the license number written into the contract itself.
  • Confirm the bond is active and note the surety company.
Watch Out Unlicensed work on projects that require permits can void your homeowner's insurance coverage for resulting damage — and can create personal liability if a worker is injured on your property.

Right #5: The 3-Day Right to Cancel

Under the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule (16 CFR Part 429), a contract of $25 or more signed at your home generally comes with a three-business-day right to cancel. The seller is required to give you two copies of a cancellation form and tell you about the right. Signed a driveway-sealing contract under pressure at your kitchen table and regretted it that night? You likely have a legal exit. Send the cancellation in writing — certified mail, return receipt requested — within the window.

Right #6: State Consumer Protection Statutes Have Teeth

All 50 states have deceptive trade practices laws. They cover false statements, bait-and-switch pricing, hidden fees, and failure to perform as contracted. Many allow recovery of attorney fees, and some allow multiplied damages for willful violations — which changes the math for a contractor deciding whether to make you whole. These statutes only work for people who can prove what happened, which brings us to the habit that protects every other right on this list.

The Habit That Makes Every Right Enforceable: Documentation

  • Every quote, change order, and promise in writing. "He said" is not evidence.
  • Photograph the work at every stage — especially anything that gets covered by drywall, concrete, or backfill.
  • Keep every receipt, canceled check, and payment record.
  • Save texts and emails. Confirm verbal conversations by follow-up text: "Confirming what we discussed today…"

Rights on paper mean nothing without proof in hand. Documentation costs you minutes. The lack of it can cost you the case.
